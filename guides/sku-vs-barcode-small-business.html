SKU vs barcode: a practical guide for small businesses

A SKU and a barcode work together, but they solve different problems. Getting that distinction right makes stock easier to find and scan, without accidentally treating an internal label as a manufacturer’s product identifier.

By the InventoryOS team · 8 November 2026

On this page

  2. A barcode makes an identifier scannable
  3. Manufacturer barcodes are a different kind of reference
  4. Decide whether to track each item or a product quantity

  6. Avoid three common mistakes

A SKU identifies the item in your own business

A stock keeping unit, or SKU, is an internal reference for a product or stock line. A reseller might use JACKET-0042; a shop might use WATER-500ML. The important part is consistency: staff should be able to tell which record they are working with even when descriptions are similar.

Decide what needs a separate record. A black medium T-shirt and a white medium T-shirt may need different SKUs because you count, price or reorder them separately. You do not need to encode every detail in the code if the product record already contains it.

A barcode makes an identifier scannable

A barcode is a machine-readable representation of data. Printing a scannable label for an internal SKU can make receiving, picking and selling faster, because a scanner enters the code instead of someone typing it. A label also needs readable text so a damaged barcode can still be checked.

Keep the identifier stable. Changing a description should not require relabelling every unit. Test a printed label using the scanner and printer combination you actually use before producing a large batch.

Manufacturer barcodes are a different kind of reference

Packaged goods often arrive with a manufacturer’s EAN barcode. It typically represents a Global Trade Item Number, or GTIN. That is different from a code your business creates for internal stock. Preserve an existing manufacturer barcode accurately when adding a shop product.

A barcode database lookup may suggest a name, but you still need to check the exact size, variant and pack. It does not know your purchase cost, selling price or physical stock. Do not turn an arbitrary SKU into a supposed official GTIN. GS1 UK’s barcode guidance explains global product identifiers.

Decide whether to track each item or a product quantity

For a one-off second-hand jacket, a separate item record makes condition, purchase cost and storage location easier to follow. For ten identical label rolls, one product record with quantity ten may be more practical. Choose based on whether the units are interchangeable in your workflow.

Also distinguish a supplier case from a sellable unit. Receiving two cases of ten should mean twenty units when the case conversion is configured. A case’s barcode should not silently replace the barcode used to sell one unit.

Use codes and locations together in InventoryOS

InventoryOS can generate internal item codes and print barcode labels representing them. Barcode label print jobs are available from Starter. Record a meaningful description and storage location as well: knowing the code is less useful if nobody knows which shelf holds it.

Start with a small test. Add one product, print and scan its label, move it to another location, then check the record before removing stock. The stock-location guide covers a naming routine you can maintain as stock grows.

Avoid three common mistakes

  • Do not reuse a code for a different product while older labels or sales still refer to it.
  • Do not assume two descriptions mean identical products; check size, colour, condition and unit quantity.
  • Do not assume printing a barcode publishes a listing or connects a marketplace automatically.

A modest, consistent system is easier to maintain than a complicated code nobody follows. If you need help with scanner input or labels, use Settings → Help or contact InventoryOS.
